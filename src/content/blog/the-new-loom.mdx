I have to admit that I'm navigating uncertainty.

I can swing from [Luddism](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddite) to techno-optimism without breaking a sweat. Which is curious, because professionally I'm up to my neck in AI. I work with it, build solutions with it, experiment with new tools, and I've been transforming the way I work for some time now.

**I'm not watching the change from the outside. I'm part of it.**

And perhaps that's precisely why I find it hard to take a definitive stance.

If I look only at the ability to write code, it's relatively easy to recognize the advantages of AI, albeit with some reservations. However, when I widen the lens and consider **the development process end to end**, the answer is no longer so obvious.

And then there are the long-term questions about how these tools, and the way we work with them, will evolve.

I don't have clear answers. It's not that I don't want to take a side; it's that I don't think I have enough data yet to justify a solid position.

For years we've built our careers around software development, acquiring knowledge and experience, and honing our ability to solve problems through code. And now, in a way, **someone has built us a new loom**.

I find that analogy particularly interesting.

The [Luddites](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Luddite) didn't simply oppose technology because it was new. Their resistance had to do with **the consequences mechanization had for their work, their skills and their economic position**.

And perhaps something similar is happening now. Part of our resistance may have less to do with the limitations of AI than with **the fear of losing a professional position that took us years to build**.

I ask myself the same question. To what extent are my reservations driven by technical and economic arguments, and to what extent are they shaped by my own experience?

There is one thing I'm fairly clear about, though: **I feel no need to defend the way we've worked until now**. If there are better ways to solve problems, I want to know them, understand them and use them. In fact, that's what I've been doing for much of my career.

But adapting shouldn't just mean learning to use new tools. We should also ask ourselves **what we're delegating to them**.

Using AI to extend our capabilities, explore alternatives or speed up tasks that used to take hours of work is one thing. **Gradually relying on its answers until we lose the ability to question them** is quite another.

And that difference strikes me as far more interesting than the debate over whether we'll keep writing code.

We could become **much more productive and, at the same time, less able to understand what we produce**. I'm not saying it will necessarily happen, but I think it's a risk worth considering.

The point isn't to keep our current skills intact, but to identify **which ones remain fundamental, which need to evolve and which we need to develop**.

Perhaps our professional value is shifting. Maybe our ability to produce code matters less and less, and our ability to **understand problems, evaluate solutions and make sound decisions** matters more.

But to play that role, we need to preserve and develop precisely **the cognitive abilities we risk delegating**.

And perhaps that is my current position. I'm neither against AI nor unconditionally in favor of it. I'm deeply involved in its development and adoption, but I'm still trying to understand all of its implications.

**Because delegating execution is one thing; delegating judgment is something else entirely.**

On this very idea, I found the preprint _Thinking—Fast, Slow, and Artificial: How AI Is Reshaping Human Reasoning and the Rise of Cognitive Surrender_, by Steven D. Shaw and Gideon Nave, particularly interesting.

The authors explore the concept of [_cognitive surrender_](https://www.economist.com/business/2026/04/30/ai-and-the-danger-of-cognitive-surrender): the tendency to accept AI's answers without subjecting them to sufficient critical reflection. I find it an interesting perspective because it suggests that the problem isn't necessarily delegating tasks, but **the extent to which we end up delegating our own judgment as well**.

It's preliminary research, but I think it adds another dimension to this reflection. Perhaps the challenge isn't only learning to work with AI, but also **learning to think alongside it without ceasing to think for ourselves**.
